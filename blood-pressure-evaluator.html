Blood Pressure Evaluator: Classify Your Reading by AHA Category, MAP & Pulse Pressure

Classify a systolic/diastolic reading using official American Heart Association categories, and see Mean Arterial Pressure and pulse pressure calculated alongside it.

Where your numbers fall on the standard categories

Blood pressure is read as two numbers — systolic (pressure during a heartbeat) over diastolic (pressure between beats) — compared against the American Heart Association's published thresholds for Normal, Elevated, Hypertension Stage 1, Hypertension Stage 2, and Hypertensive Crisis. Whichever number (systolic or diastolic) lands in the more severe category determines your overall classification.

Why a single reading isn't the full picture

Blood pressure naturally fluctuates through the day and can spike temporarily from caffeine, stress, physical activity, or the "white coat effect" of a clinical setting. A hypertension diagnosis requires an average of readings across two or more separate occasions — one elevated reading is a reason to re-check, not a diagnosis.

Blood Pressure Classification

Blood pressure measures the force of circulating blood against artery walls, expressed as two numbers in millimeters of mercury (mmHg). The systolic number (top) reflects pressure while the heart contracts and pumps blood; the diastolic number (bottom) reflects pressure while the heart rests between beats. Because sustained high blood pressure is a leading, largely symptomless risk factor for heart attack, stroke, and kidney disease, health organizations publish specific numeric thresholds so both patients and clinicians can classify a reading consistently.

What This Calculator Adds Beyond a Basic Category Lookup

  • Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP): Calculated as diastolic + 1/3 × (systolic − diastolic). MAP reflects the average pressure driving blood to your organs across a full heartbeat cycle, and is the number clinicians often watch most closely in acute care.
  • Pulse Pressure: Systolic minus diastolic. A normal pulse pressure is roughly 40 mmHg; consistently wide or narrow pulse pressure can flag arterial stiffness or reduced cardiac output.
  • Low Blood Pressure (Hypotension) Detection: Flags readings below the commonly used 90/60 mmHg threshold, which the basic AHA table doesn't address at all.
  • Isolated Systolic Hypertension Note: Flags when systolic and diastolic point to different severities, a pattern common in older adults as arteries stiffen with age.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What are the official blood pressure categories?

Per the American Heart Association: Normal is below 120/80 mmHg. Elevated is systolic 120–129 with diastolic under 80. Hypertension Stage 1 is systolic 130–139 or diastolic 80–89. Hypertension Stage 2 is systolic 140 or higher, or diastolic 90 or higher. Hypertensive Crisis is systolic over 180 and/or diastolic over 120, and requires immediate medical attention, especially with symptoms like chest pain, shortness of breath, or vision changes.

What happens if my systolic and diastolic numbers fall into different categories?

The higher category always wins. If either number qualifies for a more severe category, the reading as a whole is classified there. This is why a reading like 145/78 is still Hypertension Stage 2, even though the diastolic number looks normal — this pattern is called isolated systolic hypertension and is common in older adults.

What is Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) and why does it matter?

Mean Arterial Pressure is the average pressure in your arteries during one full heartbeat cycle, calculated as diastolic pressure plus one-third of the pulse pressure (systolic minus diastolic). A MAP of at least 60 mmHg is generally considered necessary to adequately perfuse vital organs including the brain, heart, and kidneys; clinicians often target a MAP of 65 mmHg or higher in critically ill patients.

What counts as low blood pressure (hypotension)?

There's no single universal cutoff, but a commonly used clinical threshold is systolic under 90 mmHg or diastolic under 60 mmHg. Low blood pressure is only concerning when it causes symptoms such as dizziness, fainting, blurred vision, or fatigue — a naturally low reading without symptoms isn't automatically a problem for many healthy, active people.

Can one blood pressure reading diagnose hypertension?

No. A hypertension diagnosis requires an average of two or more readings taken on two or more separate occasions, because blood pressure fluctuates naturally throughout the day and can spike temporarily from stress, caffeine, physical activity, or the "white coat effect" of being measured in a clinical setting. One elevated reading is a reason to re-check, not a diagnosis.

What is pulse pressure and what does a wide or narrow value mean?

Pulse pressure is the difference between systolic and diastolic pressure. A normal pulse pressure is roughly 40 mmHg. A consistently wide pulse pressure (over about 60 mmHg) can indicate reduced arterial elasticity, often related to aging, while a narrow pulse pressure (under about 25 mmHg) can indicate reduced cardiac output. Neither is a standalone diagnosis — both warrant discussion with a healthcare provider if persistent.

Medical Disclaimer

It is not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment, and it cannot account for medications, other health conditions, or measurement technique. Always consult a qualified healthcare provider about your blood pressure, and seek emergency care immediately for a Hypertensive Crisis reading accompanied by symptoms.
